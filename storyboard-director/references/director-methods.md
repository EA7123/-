# Director-method routing

These are analytical decision frameworks drawn from public filmmaking practice, not imitation presets. Select by the scene's dramatic problem, then record observable consequences in the Shot Cards. Use one dominant method and at most one supporting method. Reject decorative director-name stacking and claims of exact imitation.

## D00 Classical coverage

Use when clear geography, motivated performance, and a small editable set serve the beat. Establish axis and relationship, hold through behavior, add a reaction or detail only for new information, and cut on completed thought or phase-compatible action.

## D01 Staging and reveal — Spielberg studies

Use for relationships, discovery, wonder, and ensemble dialogue. Let blocking change composition within a shot; organize foreground, middle ground, and background. Reveal through entrance, occlusion, scale, or reaction before cutting. Avoid isolating every line in a close-up.

## D02 Knowledge gap and POV — Hitchcock studies

Use for suspense or withheld information. Track what character and audience know separately. Build look -> object/information -> reaction, changing order when surprise is desired. Control off-screen space and delay the confirming angle.

## D03 Dynamic composition — Kurosawa studies

Use for groups, weather, and conflicting motion. Distinguish subjects by screen direction and movement rhythm. Stage action in depth; let rain, wind, dust, fabric, or crowds clarify force. Establish geography before fragmentation.

## D04 Precision coverage — Fincher studies

Use for investigation, procedure, control, and unease. Choose exact camera positions; move only when attention changes. Favor clean eyelines, matched actions, deliberate headroom, and coverage designed for the final cut rather than quantity.

## D05 Spatial storytelling — Bong Joon-ho studies

Use when height, architecture, threshold, or access expresses power. Map stairs, levels, corridors, windows, and hidden zones. Show who can cross a boundary, and make geography legible before the reversal.

## D06 Scale and atmosphere — Villeneuve studies

Use for awe, isolation, grief, science fiction, or monumental space. Contrast human scale with negative space; favor patient reveal and sound before visual confirmation. Hold long enough for scale without substituting slowness for development.

## D07 Tactical action vectors — George Miller studies

Use for pursuits and fast physical exchanges. Establish goal, threat, and travel vector. Keep decisive movement near the viewer's attention center; cut on directional impulses without losing who pursues whom. Accelerate only after geography is clear.

## D08 Prop-led physical choreography — Jackie Chan studies

Use when an object, handoff, obstacle, or body contact drives action. Define prop owner, grip, direction, contact, reaction, and recovery. Hold wide enough to see the feat; use inserts only to clarify the cause or result.

## D09 Relational action and opposing motion — John Woo studies

Use when action expresses loyalty, rivalry, or sacrifice. Keep both sides' intentions and sightlines readable; contrast movement direction and reaction holds. Choreograph around a relationship change, not impact decoration.

## D10 Restrained emotional exchange — Ang Lee studies

Use for conflict beneath polite or withheld dialogue. Let posture, distance, hands, and eyelines reveal what a line conceals. Hold a two-shot when the relationship matters; choose a close view for a specific decision or loss of control.

## D11 Ensemble observation — Edward Yang studies

Use for layered group behavior and social distance. Stage in depth; let entrances and background behavior change a foreground exchange. Keep stable geography so the audience can choose where to look.

## D12 Animated visual transition — Satoshi Kon studies

Use for subjective shifts, memory, or dreams. Match a shape, gesture, sound, or screen position across the transition; define what remains stable. Do not disorient a literal action that must stay clear.

## D13 Lived-in environment and motion — Miyazaki studies

Use when setting and everyday action carry emotion. Give wind, water, cloth, plants, and pauses physical causes and varied rhythms. Let characters interact with the place rather than merely pose in front of it.

## D14 Color, ritual, and group geometry — Zhang Yimou studies

Use for ceremony, power display, and collective movement. Give color and formation a story function, then change the pattern at a reversal. Keep the character's small action visible inside spectacle.

## D15 Subjective time and emotional distance — Wong Kar-wai studies

Use for longing, avoidance, recollection, or missed contact. Separate objective event from felt duration; use a held angle, obstruction, or sound bridge to express distance. Do not blur space or slow dialogue by default.

## D16 Vertical wuxia geography — Tsui Hark studies

Use for aerial movement, uneven terrain, multi-level combat, or rapid entrances. Map floor, ledge, roof, and flight path before dynamic camera moves. Show launch, travel, contact, landing, and recovery.

## Routing examples

- Quiet two-person climb and helping action: D01 or D10 dominant; D05 can support the height/target reveal. D08 is appropriate when grasp/pull mechanics drive the scene.
- Physical fight with props: D08 or D07 dominant; D03 for weather or group motion, D16 for vertical wuxia space.
- Dialogue with concealed knowledge: D02 or D10 dominant; D11 for ensemble geography.
- Stylized subjective animation: D12 dominant only when a subjective shift exists; D13 for lived-in surroundings.
- Ceremony or monumental reveal: D14 or D06 dominant, according to whether group geometry or scale drives the beat.

Record the selected method ID, reason, and at least two observable consequences before Shot Card design. Each Shot Card carries its method impact. Keep lens policy, contrast, movement, and pacing coherent across adjacent scenes.
